CS180 Project 3A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

Part 1: Visualizing Training and Sampling

1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

To get a noisy training input, I linearly interpolate between Gaussian noise ε and the clean image x1: xt = (1 − t)ε + t·x1. t = 0 is pure noise and t = 1 is the original image, so a larger t keeps more of the original. I used a photo I took of Washington Square Park and reused the same noise sample for all three noise levels.

1.2 Classical Denoising

I divided each noisy input by t to restore the image's brightness, then applied a Gaussian blur. I tested σ = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and kept the one with the lowest error to the clean image, which was σ = 8, 2, and 1 for the three noise levels.

t = 0.25 (σ = 8)

t = 0.5 (σ = 2)

t = 0.75 (σ = 1)

At t = 0.25 there's so much noise that it took a really heavy blur to get rid of it, and all that was left was a colorful blob. At lower noise a smaller blur was enough, but the results were still soft and speckly, since blurring removes detail along with the noise.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

I used PixNerd with the prompt "a high quality photo" to predict the velocity once, then estimated the clean image with x̂1 = xt + (1 − t)·v̂.

This is way better than blurring. Even at t = 0.25 the arch, trees, and people come back, though the result is pretty smooth and painterly.

1.4 Visualizing Velocity

Since I know the original image and the noise, I know the exact update the model should add: (1 − t)(x1 − ε). I compared it with the predicted update (1 − t)v̂ and plotted the RMS difference across RGB at each pixel. All updates share one color scale (gray = 0), and the error maps share the same limits.

Original image

The error is biggest at t = 0.25 and fades as t increases. It shows up mostly on fine details like the leaves, the arch's outline, and the crowd, which the model can't know exactly, so it predicts a smoother version instead.

1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Instead of jumping straight to the end, Euler sampling takes small steps (xt+h = xt + h·v(xt, t)) and predicts a new velocity after each one. Starting from my t = 0.5 input, I took 25 steps of h = 0.02. These are the initial image, every fifth update, and the final result.

Euler looks the best by far: the leaves have real texture and the people are sharp. Interestingly, it had a slightly higher MSE than one-step (0.028 vs. 0.025, compared to 0.055 for blur). One-step predicts an average, which is blurrier but closer pixel-by-pixel, while Euler commits to one sharp image whose details don't line up exactly with the real photo.

1.6 Generating Images from Noise

Starting Euler sampling at t = 0 from pure noise generates an image from scratch. These use seeds 180–184, the prompt "a high quality photo", and 50 steps.

The generic prompt mostly produces portraits, but several of them are distorted (seed 180 doesn't even have a face).

Part 2: Conditional Generation and Guidance

2.1 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

CFG runs the model twice at each step, once with the prompt and once with an empty prompt, and pushes further in the direction the prompt adds: vcfg = vc + w(vc − vu). These use w = 7 and the same five noises as above.

The faces are much cleaner and more realistic, but the images are a lot less diverse. They all look like headshots from the same studio photo shoot.

Part 3: Creative Applications

3.1 Image-to-Image Editing

I noised each image to a starting time and then sampled with CFG from there to 1, using "a high quality photo". Smaller starting times add more noise, so the model has to make up more and the edit gets bigger. All edits use starting times 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.10, 0.14, 0.25, and 0.50.

3.3 Visual Anagrams

At each step, I get the CFG velocity for the first prompt on the image, and the CFG velocity for the second prompt on the image flipped upside down. I flip the second one back, average the two, and take an Euler step. This gives an image that looks like one thing upright and another upside down.

3.4 Hybrid Images

At each step I combine the low frequencies of one prompt's CFG velocity with the high frequencies of another's: v = lowpass(va) + vb − lowpass(vb). The low-pass is a Gaussian blur with σ = 16 and a 265 × 265 kernel. Up close you see the high-frequency prompt, and from far away (the small version) you see the low-frequency one.

CS180 Project 3B: Flow Matching from Scratch!

Part 1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

1.1 & 1.2 Implementing the UNet and Visualizing Noise

I built a UNet with downsampling and upsampling blocks connected by skip connections. The denoiser learns to map a noisy image z = x + σε back to the clean digit x. Here are MNIST digits (scaled to [0, 1]) at σ = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0.

1.2.1 Training

I trained a D = 128 UNet for 5 epochs at σ = 0.5, with batch size 256 and Adam (lr = 1e-4). I added fresh noise to every batch so the model never saw the same noisy image twice.

Even after 1 epoch the denoised digits look pretty close to the originals. After 5 epochs the strokes are cleaner with smoother edges.

1.2.2 Out-of-Distribution Testing

I kept the same test digits and noise pattern and varied σ. Odd rows are noisy inputs and even rows are the denoised outputs.

The model does well at or below σ = 0.5, but at 0.8 and 1.0 it starts adding stray strokes and making up digit shapes, since it was only trained on one noise level.

1.2.3 Denoising Pure Noise

I trained a fresh model the same way, but with pure noise as the input and clean digits as the target.

Every output is the same blurry blob that looks like a bunch of digits stacked on top of each other. Since pure noise tells the model nothing about which digit is the target, the best it can do under MSE loss is output the average of all the training digits.

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

2.1 – 2.2 Time Conditioning and Training

I added time conditioning to a D = 64 UNet using two fully-connected blocks, and trained it to predict the velocity x1 − x0 from xt = (1 − t)x0 + t·x1, with a random t for each image. I trained for 10 epochs with batch size 64 and Adam, starting at lr = 1e-2 with exponential decay by 0.11/10 each epoch.

2.3 Sampling from the UNet

2.4 – 2.5 Class Conditioning and Training

I added the digit label as a one-hot vector, passed through two more fully-connected blocks, and dropped it 10% of the time so the model also learns to generate without a class. Training used the same settings as the time-only model.

2.6 Sampling from the UNet

I sampled with 300 Euler steps and classifier-free guidance u = uuncond + 5(ucond − uuncond). Each row is digits 0–9, with four samples per digit, and I reused the same noise across epochs.

Removing the learning rate scheduler

I trained a separate model with a constant learning rate of 1e-3 instead of starting at 1e-2 and decaying it. Since 1e-3 is where the scheduler ends up, the lower rate keeps training stable without needing to decay.
